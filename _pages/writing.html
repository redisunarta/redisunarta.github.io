SEA Subsidy Wars 2018–2022

Series 1 · 12 parts · complete
Part 1The Rise and Fall of Burn Rate $36 billion burned across Southeast Asian platforms in four years. Nobody could stop unilaterally, and nobody ran a control group. Part 2When the discount stops, who actually leaves and why? Price elasticity is the wrong mechanism. Users judge the new price against the subsidised one they were trained on. Part 3Switching cost, not subsidies, determined the outcome Infrastructure subsidies outlast product subsidies, because only one of them changes what it costs to leave. Part 4GoFood lost 8 points; GrabFood lost 2. Why does that matter? Merchant density is a moat. Consumer discounts are not — and the gap shows up the moment spending stops. Part 5Trapped seller: from 0% to 10% commission The subsidy’s sign flips. Merchants who joined to be paid stay to pay fees, because leaving now costs more than the fee. Part 6Driver supply stayed, when wages were cut Supply held after pay fell, which looks like resilience and is really adverse selection. The welfare cost went uncounted. Part 7When the cash cow dies, the conglomerate model breaks Garena funded Shopee. That cross-subsidy, not strategy, explains who could burn longest and who had to stop. Part 8Lazada: the cost of not playing The counterfactual nobody volunteered to run. Sitting out the subsidy war turned out to have its own price. Part 9Indonesia’s TikTok ban strengthened Shopee, not Tokopedia The cleanest natural experiment of the period. Regulation moved market share in a way capital never managed to. Part 10Income distribution and true market size Indonesia’s platform market is capped by income, not awareness. The addressable base is far smaller than the population. Part 11Who can restart the subsidy wars, and when The conditions under which burning cash becomes rational again — and the short list of players who could meet them. Part 12After the burn: how the survivors actually make money now Where the margin came from once the discounts stopped, and which side of the marketplace ended up paying for it.

The Great Extraction

Series 2 · 7 parts · complete

The five ways Southeast Asia’s platforms make money — take-rate, ads, lending, cross-sell and deposits — and the outside force that caps each one.

Part 1The superapp myth: more products actually shrink RPU GoTo runs five business lines and earns $4.92 per user a quarter; Grab runs fewer and earns $18.46. Breadth lifts none of the ceilings. Part 2What sets the ceiling on marketplace commission? Shopee went from zero to 12%. The ceiling is what a seller forfeits by leaving, not market share. Build the exit cost first, raise the price second. Part 3Sellers now pay 25–30% of marketplace revenue just to be seen Ads are placement sellers used to get free. Every new bidder raises cost per click while sales per click stay flat, so returns fall by arithmetic. Part 4Who is Indonesia’s fintech loan growth actually lending to? Fintech lenders provisioned 4.3% for losses, double the banks. Defaults lag approvals by 18 months, so loosening looks profitable inside a quarter. Part 5A banking licence doesn’t automatically create borrowers SeaBank paid 6% while BCA paid 2.25%. Deposits arrive on day one; borrowers don’t. A deposit you can’t lend is a daily expense. Part 6Zero MDR barely dented payment revenue — that’s exactly why it worked Bank Indonesia could afford zero QRIS fees because fees follow value. The platforms kept the money and lost the lock; only lending still holds anyone. Part 7GoTo’s fintech hit 48% of profit. Five quarters ago it was 12% Every superapp is running to lending. Know which of the five levers carries your margin — and who else gets to set its price.
